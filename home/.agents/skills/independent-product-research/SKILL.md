# Independent product research

Recommend the product that best fits the user's use case at an acceptable level of uncertainty. Keep expected fit separate from confidence in the evidence: a well-tested product is not automatically the best product, and an insufficiently tested product is not automatically poor.

## Establish the decision

Extract the decision brief from what the user has already provided. Ask only about unresolved choices likely to change the shortlist or recommendation:

- intended use and usage intensity;
- must-haves and deal-breakers;
- budget and whether it is a hard ceiling;
- purchase country and currency;
- purchase deadline;
- acceptable condition, such as new, refurbished, or used;
- acceptable compromises.

If material ambiguity leaves two or more substantially different recommendation paths, state a concise brief and ask the user to confirm the missing decision. Otherwise, state any reasonable assumptions and proceed.

Scale the research effort to the purchase price, consequences of failure, expected ownership period, ease of return, and available evidence. A low-cost reversible purchase does not require the same depth as an expensive, safety-critical, or long-lived product.

## Keep evidence roles separate

Use each source only for the role it can support.

### Evaluation evidence

Use independent hands-on tests, measurements, teardowns, long-term tests, repairability assessments, and comparative analyses to judge performance and usability. Prefer sources that:

- disclose their method and test conditions;
- identify the exact tested model, revision, and firmware when relevant;
- report observable results rather than repeat product claims;
- test competing products under comparable conditions;
- distinguish measurements from impressions.

Two sources count as independent only when they performed their own evaluation and do not merely repeat the same underlying test, press material, or syndicated content. Affiliate funding or a manufacturer-supplied sample is a potential conflict, not an automatic disqualification; retain the source when its method and results remain inspectable, and disclose conflicts that materially affect confidence.

### Product facts

Use manufacturer manuals, datasheets, support pages, warranty terms, compatibility lists, firmware notes, and regulatory filings for specifications, policies, and documented compatibility. Treat manufacturer performance claims as claims unless independent testing verifies them.

### Market facts

Use retailers, marketplaces, authorized-dealer lists, and price-comparison services for current price, availability, seller identity, condition, return terms, warranty coverage, and package contents. Record the date checked and compare the total delivered price, including taxes, shipping, required accessories, and import costs when relevant.

### Owner-reported signals

Use owner reviews and forums to identify possible recurring defects, compatibility problems, and long-term failure modes. Give a pattern more weight when the same specific symptom appears across different authors and platforms and matches the relevant model or revision. Treat these reports as risk signals, not failure-rate estimates, unless reliable denominator data exists.

### Audit source eligibility

Before synthesizing results, classify every source that affects the conclusion or could reasonably appear to support it:

- **accepted** — the source performed its own relevant evaluation and its method and findings are sufficiently inspectable;
- **partially used** — the source contains usable findings, but its overall score, ranking, provenance, or method mixes evidence roles or remains unclear;
- **excluded** — the source did not perform a relevant evaluation, merely republishes another source, or provides no support suitable for the claim.

For a partially used source, preserve its published result as context, state the concrete limitation, and identify exactly which findings remain usable. A mixed or opaque score is not automatically worthless, but keep the overall score or rank out of a quality consensus when it blends quality with price, popularity, sales, clicks, customer ratings, review count, availability, brand reputation, or undisclosed factors. Describe the limitation rather than alleging deception without evidence.

When evaluation evidence cannot distinguish otherwise suitable candidates, a popularity-related signal may be used as an explicitly labeled, low-confidence tie-breaker for the purchase decision. State what the signal is being used as a proxy for, why that proxy matters to the user's use case, and whether it changes the recommendation. It remains a market or owner-reported signal; it does not become evaluation evidence or an independent vote in a quality consensus.

## Build the shortlist from evidence

Begin with credible comparative testing to understand the tested field, then widen the search when necessary to include newer, niche, or use-case-specific candidates. Do not choose candidates from sales rank, customer-star averages, advertising prominence, or untested roundups.

Keep the serious shortlist proportionate to the market; three to five candidates is usually enough. For every serious candidate:

- match the exact model, generation, region, condition, and relevant hardware or firmware revision;
- record what was tested, what was documented, and what was merely asserted;
- verify decision-critical specifications against first-party documentation;
- verify the current local offer and exact package separately from product quality;
- check required consumables, subscriptions, replacement parts, accessories, and ecosystem costs when they could affect the decision;
- note the test date and any known later revision or firmware change that could alter the result.

Transfer test results between variants only after establishing that the relevant hardware, software, and tested behavior are equivalent. Differences limited to color, plug, storage tier, or bundled accessories may be immaterial, but state the mapping when it affects confidence.

Label a candidate **insufficiently tested** when credible evaluation evidence is absent. Keep it as a lower-confidence option if its expected fit remains strong; do not infer quality from specifications, brand reputation, sales volume, or customer-star averages.

## Compare for the confirmed use case

Set priorities from the decision brief before selecting the winner. Compare candidates on the behavior that matters to the user, including measured performance, ergonomics, compatibility, support, repairability, and ownership cost. Treat extra features as neutral unless they solve a stated need.

Avoid numerical scoring when the evidence does not support precise weights. Explain decisive trade-offs directly.

For ownership cost, use the user's expected ownership period and usage level. If these are unknown but material, state a simple scenario rather than presenting a context-free total.

Separate reliability evidence into:

- durability observed in long-term or stress testing;
- recurring owner-reported failure signals;
- known failure-rate or service data;
- warranty, repairability, parts availability, and support.

Do not convert short-term testing or anecdotal reports into unsupported claims about failure frequency.

Maintain an internal claim ledger for claims that affect inclusion, ranking, compatibility, reliability, ownership cost, price, or the final recommendation. Each such claim needs a source suited to its evidence role. When credible sources disagree, check whether method, revision, firmware, environment, or sample variation explains the difference. Preserve the uncertainty when it cannot be resolved and explain which evidence carries more weight and why.

## Stop when the decision is supported

Research is sufficient when:

- the decision-changing requirements are settled;
- the realistic market has been sampled and the serious candidates have been checked against the brief;
- decision-critical specifications and compatibility are verified;
- the leading candidate has two genuinely independent evaluation sources when adequate coverage exists;
- its current local configuration, seller, package, and total price are verified;
- further searching is unlikely to change the recommendation or reveal a major unexamined risk.

If only one credible evaluation exists, lower confidence and say so. If no candidate has adequate evidence within the constraints, state that plainly and propose the smallest useful relaxation instead of manufacturing certainty.

## Deliver the recommendation

Match the length of the report to the purchase's cost, risk, and complexity. Include:

1. the requirements, assumptions, market, and date checked;
2. a source-audit table listing each material source, its type, status (**accepted**, **partially used**, or **excluded**), usable evidence, and the reason for any limitation;
3. a compact comparison of the finalists that separates measured findings from specifications and owner-reported signals;
4. one clear recommendation and the exact model, revision, size, capacity, or bundle to buy;
5. why it fits the stated use and the confidence level of the conclusion;
6. alternatives tied to specific trade-offs;
7. the current total price, seller, condition, warranty status, and package contents, kept separate from the quality judgment;
8. material caveats, evidence gaps, and only the rejected candidates that were close contenders or likely traps;
9. direct links next to the claims they support.

When useful, give price thresholds that would change the choice, such as choosing one candidate below a stated price and another above it. State what new evidence or market change would alter the conclusion.
